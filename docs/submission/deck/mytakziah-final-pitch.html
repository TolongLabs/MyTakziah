1. The Problem 2 / 7

Malaysia is ageing. The paperwork
after a death has not changed.

1
8.4 per cent of Malaysians are now 65 or over, up from 8.0 per cent a year earlier.
That is roughly 2.7 million people, and the share is rising every year. 198,992 people died in Malaysia in 2024, and the median age at death is 69.
2
Three agencies then ask that family for the same five things, separately.
JPN, PERKESO and KWSP each demand the same core information again, on three different forms, and mostly in person with original documents.
3
Two of the three agencies enforce deadlines they do not fully publish.
The person left holding that paperwork is usually an older widow or widower. Miss the EPF window and the RM2,500 Death Assistance is gone.

Three different forms: JPN.LM02, KWSP 9KM (AHL), and Borang 34 or Borang 24 and 26.
Parliament recorded 72,929 late death registrations between 2003 and 2014, and named funeral rites and religious obligations as the reason families could not meet the deadline. Published accounts describe survivors collecting benefits more than twenty years late, simply because nobody told them.
2. Our Solution 3 / 7

Provide once. We prepare every claim.

1
Provide once.
Identity, proof of death, relationship and bank details, entered a single time.
2
Every entitlement, with the government's own rule printed beside it.
Ranked by deadline, soonest first, never by value.
3
Submitted, receipted and tracked. Never approved.
The agency always keeps the decision. We prepare and we file. We never decide who qualifies.

3. What Makes It Innovative 4 / 7

Not a chatbot. A filing engine
with the rulebook attached.

1
JPN has already built a chatbot. It still cannot reach KWSP.
A chatbot tells you what to do. MyTakziah does it. That is the whole difference, and it is why this has not been solved already.
2
Deadline first, and every rule cited to its source.
Where an agency publishes no deadline, we say so on the screen instead of guessing. Nothing we show a family is invented.
3
Routed to the family's own rites.
The checklist a Muslim family sees is not the one a Chinese or Hindu family sees. Religion changes the documents, the timing and the services that matter.
Every entitlement carries its deadline, its source link, and a plain statement of what the agency does not publish.
Other countries have built the notification half of this. The UK's Tell Us Once tells departments that someone has died; it does not file the claim or protect the deadline. None of it is built for JPN, KWSP and PERKESO. Our advantage is not the technology, because anyone can add AI. It is that we read these three agencies' forms until we understood this ecosystem better than a general purpose assistant ever will.

3
Integration is proposed, not proven.
Until an agency connects we print the completed forms and the family carries them. They still know what they are owed and which deadline is first.
Three forms prepared from one set of evidence, and one timeline.
Where we are today: a working prototype, and rules verified against primary government documents.  ·  Next: a minimum viable product, then one community pilot.
5. Impact and Potential 6 / 7

Three sides benefit.
One of them can pay.

1
Families use it free. Always.
They are the people least able to pay, at the worst possible moment. Charging them would defeat the point.
2
Government gets complete, on time, correctly filled submissions.
Fewer rejected forms, fewer repeat counter visits, and deaths registered inside the window rather than years late.
3
Service providers pay to reach families at the moment of need.
Funeral, memorial and estate services already spend to find these families. A listing and referral model funds the platform without ever charging the bereaved.
Accountable institutions, and social protection
that actually reaches the people entitled to it.
RM3.24 billion moved through EPF death withdrawals in 2024, across 85,532 cases.  ·  We will measure families served, agencies connected and providers onboarded.  ·  Malaysia first, then the same problem across Southeast Asia.
6. Why Us, and What We Are Asking For 7 / 7

We already built it.
We need the doors.

1
Two computer science students and two actuarial science students.
The eligibility rules, contribution tests and deadlines behind this are actuarial work, and we did it ourselves rather than guessing at it.
2
Every number here is verified against primary government documents.
Where we could not verify something we left it off the slide. We would rather be narrow than wrong.
3
What we need is not mainly money. It is access.
We do not reach families by advertising. We reach one community through the people already trusted with a death there, and learn from it before going wider. Introduce us to one, and to service providers, and we will have real users inside a month.
